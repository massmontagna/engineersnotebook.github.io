Reynolds number

The Reynolds number compares a flow's inertia with its viscosity. In a pipe it predicts whether the flow is smooth and layered or chaotic:

Re =ρ·v·Dμ

Worked example

Uses the values in Fig. 1.3-1. Change them and the working updates.

Given
  • ρwater at 20 °C998 kg/m³
  • μdynamic viscosity1 × 10⁻³ Pa·s
  • vmean speed0.03 m/s
  • Dpipe diameter50 mm
Find

The Reynolds number, and whether the flow is laminar or turbulent.

Solution
Re = ρvD/μ = 998 kg/m³ × 0.03 m/s × 0.05 m / 1 × 10⁻³ Pa·s = 1,497
Answer
Re = 1,497

Pipe flow is laminar below about 2,300 and turbulent above about 4,000, so this flow is laminar.

Assumptions
  • The thresholds, laminar below about 2,300 and turbulent above about 4,000, are for fully developed flow in smooth, straight, round pipes.
  • Transition is not sharp. Very smooth, undisturbed pipes can stay laminar well above 2,300.
  • Fluid properties are typical values at 20 °C. Viscosity changes strongly with temperature, especially for oils and glycerol.

Derivation
  1. Inertial force on a parcel of fluid scales as dynamic pressure times area: ρv² · D².
  2. Viscous force scales as shear stress times area: (μv/D) · D² = μvD.
  3. Their ratio is ρv²D² / μvD = ρvD/μ.

When Re is large, inertia wins and small disturbances grow into turbulence. When it is small, viscosity damps them out.

Related forms

On a flat plate, transition to turbulence starts near Rex ≈ 5 × 10⁵.

  • With kinematic viscosity

    Re = vDν

  • Flat plate, distance x along it

    Rex = ρvxμ

  • Non-circular duct

    Dh = 4AP
