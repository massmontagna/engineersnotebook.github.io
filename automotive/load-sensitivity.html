Tyre load sensitivity

Press a tyre harder into the road and it grips harder, but not in proportion: its friction coefficient falls as the load rises. So when cornering moves load from the inner wheel to the outer, the outer gains less grip than the inner loses, and the axle as a whole grips less:

Worked example

Uses the values in Fig. 3.2-1. Change them and the working updates.

Given
  • Fz0static wheel load4 kN
  • ΔWlateral load transfer2 kN
  • μ0friction coefficient at zero load1.3
  • kload sensitivity0.04 /kN
Find

The grip the axle loses because its wheel loads are unequal.

Solution
Finner = (μ0 − kFz)Fz = (1.3 − 0.04 × 2) × 2 kN = 2.44 kN Fouter = (1.3 − 0.04 × 6) × 6 kN = 6.36 kN Equal loads: 2 × (1.3 − 0.04 × 4) × 4 kN = 9.12 kN ΔF = 9.12 kN − 8.8 kN = 0.32 kN
Answer
ΔF = 0.32 kN

The same as 2kΔW² = 2 × 0.04 × 2² = 0.32 kN, whatever the static load.

Assumptions
  • The friction coefficient falls in a straight line with load. Real tyres follow a curve, but a straight line fits well over the working range.
  • Both tyres on the axle are the same and are both at their grip limit.
  • Camber, tyre temperature and pressure are ignored.
  • The model holds below the load of peak grip, μ0/2k. Above it, the straight line would predict less force for more load.

Derivation
  1. One tyre's peak lateral force is F = μFz = μ0Fz − kFz², a curve that bends over as load rises.
  2. Cornering moves ΔW from the inner wheel to the outer: Fz = Fz0 ∓ ΔW.
  3. Adding the two tyres: Faxle = 2μ0Fz0 − 2kFz0² − 2kΔW².
  4. The first two terms are the grip with equal loads, so the transfer costs ΔF = 2kΔW².

The loss grows with the square of the transfer and does not depend on the static load. This is how roll stiffness tunes the balance of a car: stiffening one axle's anti-roll bar moves more of the transfer onto that axle, which then runs out of grip first. Stiffer at the front gives understeer; stiffer at the rear, oversteer.
