Poisson's ratio

Stretch a bar and it gets thinner; squash it and it bulges. Poisson's ratio is the lateral strain per unit axial strain:

Worked example

Uses the values in Fig. 1.2-1. Change them and the working updates.

Given
  • νsteel0.3
  • εaxaxial strain1%
  • doriginal width20 mm
Find

The lateral strain and the change in width.

Solution
εlat = −νεax = −0.3 × 1% = −0.3% Δd = εlatd = −3 × 10⁻³ × 20 mm = −0.06 mm
Answer
εlat = −0.3%

The width changes by −0.06 mm, and the volume by 0.4%.

Assumptions
  • The material is isotropic and linear-elastic, and strains are small.
  • The stress is uniaxial: the sides are free to move.
  • For isotropic materials −1 < ν ≤ 0.5. At 0.5 the volume never changes, which rubber nearly achieves.
  • Values are typical. Cork's ratio is close to zero, which is why a cork goes into a bottle without bulging.

Derivation
  1. Apply axial strain ε. Each lateral direction then strains by −νε.
  2. A block of volume V becomes V(1 + ε)(1 − νε)².
  3. Expand and drop the squared terms, which are tiny for small strains: V(1 + ε − 2νε).
  4. So ΔV/V = ε(1 − 2ν).

With ν = 0.5 the volume stays constant, which is why rubber is nearly incompressible yet easy to stretch.

Related elastic constants

  • Shear modulus

    E = 2G(1 + ν)

  • Bulk modulus

    K = E3(1 − 2ν)

  • Volume change, uniaxial

    ΔVV = εax(1 − 2ν)
